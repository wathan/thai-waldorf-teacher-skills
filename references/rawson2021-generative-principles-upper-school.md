# Generative Principles for Upper-School Instructional Design (Rawson 2021)

> **Source:** Martyn Rawson, *Steiner Waldorf Pedagogy in Schools: A Critical Introduction* (Routledge, 2021). Main sources: Part 2 “Generative principles of teaching and learning” (pp. 50–131) and Part 3 “Communities of learning” (pp. 132–157); also Part 1, pp. 42–45 (developmental phases). Page numbers are the book’s printed pages. File: `sources/rawson2021steiner.pdf`.

Short quotes are marked “…” with page numbers. Points carried from earlier grades are kept only where they still govern upper-school design.

## 0. How to use generative principles

- They are **heuristics or “living theories”**, not a method or a script (p. 51). Use them to **plan, self-evaluate, evaluate schools, develop teachers and do research** (p. 51).
- Waldorf method is **procedural**: start from the developmental anthropology, keep the core aims in view, and **choose pathways that fit *these* learners in *this* context** (pp. 50–51). → An agent should *compose* each lesson for its context, never fill a fixed template.
- “The onus is on teachers to be creative … read the given situation, form judgements and plan the lesson accordingly” (p. 50). Novices may follow school practice. Experienced teachers adapt (p. 145).
- Curriculum is layered (P17). **Macro** = universal developmental themes. **Meso** = locally adapted topics and skill progressions, where Thai standards sit. **Micro** = actual lesson plans. Generated lessons are micro-level and must trace up to meso and macro.

## 1. Developmental frame for the upper school

| Feature | Design consequence |
|---|---|
| Puberty is a “third birth”: the psyche individualises (p. 42). Mode of learning shifts from authority to **personal judgement** (p. 45). | Design for students *forming and justifying their own judgements*. Do not hand them pre-formed conclusions. |
| Steiner says little about this phase except “the emergence of the ability to discern and form judgements” and the teacher guiding students to become **independent thinkers** (p. 44). | Every block needs moments where students take an individual position. |
| Upper-school pedagogy centres on **transformative learning and forming the person through biographical intentions, interests and potential** (Zech 2018, p. 94). | Choose topics and framings that connect to adolescents’ identity questions and ideals (“the search for higher meaning and identity”, p. 71). |
| After puberty, sleep processes the day “in terms of their relevance to her biographical intentions” (p. 87). Young people are drawn to what matters to them (Dreier). | Present material so it can resonate with personal concerns. Leave room for that resonance overnight. |
| Adolescents must be able to identify with their own individuality to engage their will. Fulfilling projects, initiatives and creative work help them overcome egotism, narcissism and fixations, transforming egotism into “love and interest in humanity, the world and the environment” (Wiehl & Zech, p. 88). | Include agentic work: projects, initiatives, creative production. Aim to widen interest outward to the world. |
| High-school learning happens with **subject specialists in “hybrid practices”**: the lab, studio or workshop is a school version of a real community of practice, and the teacher practises the field (pp. 100, 133). | Frame students as doing physics, history or art the way practitioners do, with the methods, tools and standards of the discipline. |
| Teaching style changes *significantly* with and after puberty (p. 101). Development varies widely; age stages are ideal types set by the teaching, not norms (pp. 144–145). | Treat grade descriptors (e.g. the macro-curriculum reference) as orientation, not prescription. Differentiate (P12). |
| Attunement still matters in the high school (singing, recitation), but “alert interest through self-activity is more important than routine” (p. 82). | Keep openings short and purposeful. The opening’s job is to wake up interest. |

## 2. The learning process (core engine of all lesson design)

### 2.1 Seven phases of learning (P5, Fig. 2.2, p. 85)

1. **Taking in rich experience.** Notice, attend, encounter. Ideally with anticipation, curiosity and wonder. The encounter may **interrupt** existing views: surprising, puzzling, even shocking (p. 86).
2. **Forgetting.** Leave **≥24 hours** before recall. Where possible, *do not discuss or analyse the experience in the same lesson* (p. 86). Sleep consolidates. Experiences the teacher has anticipated and emphasised, and those tied to social belonging, get priority. Stress hurts consolidation (p. 87).
3. **Recalling, sharing, clarifying.** Each student actively reconstructs the experience. Use **open questions, not a test** (p. 90). Share, compare, fill gaps, build a multi-perspective picture (p. 90).
4. **Making meaning, constructing concepts.** Move from *I recall* to *we recall* to the features we jointly judge most relevant. Name, define, document. Co-constructed concepts are individualised and remembered. *Afterwards* the teacher may add the technical terms, definitions and formulas scientists use (p. 90). Loebell calls the result a moment of “self-evidence” (p. 90).
5. **Practising and applying.** Repeat with variation, in increasingly unfamiliar authentic settings. Redraft after feedback. Learn from mistakes. Apply playfully to new contexts (p. 92).
6. **Growing abilities.** Long-term embodied dispositions, consolidated by repeated block cycles and fallow periods. Needs scaffolding and assessment for learning (p. 93).
7. **Transformative learning.** Change in understanding of world, self and others. Self-formation and subjectification (p. 93).

- **Depth:** phase 1 is surface learning. Phases 3–4 move to deep or relational learning. Deep learning then requires iterative application (pp. 91–92, after Hattie).
- **Reflection** belongs to recall. Use criteria students understand, keep it age-sensitive and aim it at “the will to do better”. Three steps: what happened vs what should have happened → differences and surprises → personal reflection on what to do better next time (p. 91).
- **Share and exhibit outcomes** (collective achievement + self-efficacy) rather than awarding prizes (p. 91).

### 2.2 Upper-school version (Zech 2018, cited pp. 97, 106)

1. **Taking in** new material or experience.
2. **Working process:** opening up, interrogating, analysing, structuring, ordering, relating.
3. **Forming concepts:** taking an *individual position*, then integrating it into the general understanding of the theme.

- Steps 2–3 can sit in **one 40–50-minute process**, aiming at **individual insight and self-positioning, not a predetermined concept** (p. 97).
- Trust students to identify what is relevant, reduce complexity to essentials, observe exactly and approach the phenomena themselves (Sommer, p. 97).
- **Characterise rather than define.** Use dialogue so students become capable of judging in dialogue. Their own judgements prevent premature closure (p. 97).

### 2.3 Three stages of knowing (P10, pp. 106–107, 110–112)

| Stage | Steiner (Schluss → Urteil → Begriff) | Body / stance | Perspective (Fuchs/Sommer, Fig. 2.4) |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 Encounter → experience | “taking a stance” in perception | feet / legs / will | lived body; first-person, centred, participatory |
| 2 Engage relationally | weighing up, dialogue, judgement | arms / hands / feeling | oscillates between the two perspectives |
| 3 Living concept | grasping lawfulness and wider context | head / thinking | physical body; eccentric, objectifying, reflective |

- Concepts are **living**: provisional and able to grow (p. 104, 107).
- Concepts must be **brought forth by students’ own agency, guided**, not delivered pre-packed (p. 109).

### 2.4 Rhythm across days (P6, Fig. 2.3, pp. 95–96)

Each experience E*n* runs through: day 1 **experience** → day 2 **recall** → day 3 **concept** → day 4 **apply**. New experiences enter daily, so the cycles overlap. A steady-state main lesson day therefore holds:

```
Recall E(n-1)  →  Understand/concept E(n-2)  →  New experience E(n)  →  Apply C(n-3)
```

- On a block’s day 1 there is nothing to recall, so connect to the previous block in this subject (p. 95).
- In higher grades, recall can flow straight into discussion and concept formation, per Zech (p. 97).
- **Block frame:** preview at the start (where the journey goes) and a summary of key ideas at the end. Year-end recapitulation links blocks (p. 100).

### 2.5 Within-lesson rhythm (P4, P11, pp. 81–82, 117–119)

- Balance **active ↔ quiet, speaking ↔ listening, pair/group ↔ alone, close attention ↔ relaxed**. Mood moves between seriousness, reverence and humour. Include moments of being touched or moved (p. 82).
- **End state:** students have worked hard and leave **energised and fulfilled, not drained or over-excited** (p. 82).
- **Sonata form** (Wiechert): exposition → development → intensified reprise → coda. The coda can come the next day after recall (p. 117).
- **Structure visible, not a rigid time plan.** Have alternatives ready. Lessons need both choreography and improvisation (p. 119).
- **Typical sequence:** greeting/verse → attunement → recall → new experience → individual or group work → close (p. 82). The upper-school vignettes (pp. 59–63) add these elements:
  - student-led song or poems
  - a student news report linked to the theme
  - group check-ins on projects
  - mental reconstruction of yesterday’s image in pairs
  - a new input or task
  - quiet portfolio or writing time with individual teacher feedback

## 3. The principles: rule → upper-school design implications → check

### P1 The spiritual dimension is taken seriously (pp. 64–72)

- **Rule:** Promote no worldview. *Do* cultivate a relationship to the spiritual: a sense of being part of a greater whole, wonder, reverence, intrinsic meaning. Leave students free in how they respond (pp. 64–65).
- **Design:**
  - Teach phenomena “for their own sake”, not only because they are useful. Appreciate qualities (colour, form, living creatures, mathematical properties) and not only “building blocks to useful, technocratic knowledge” (p. 70).
  - Think in processes, relationships, connections and rhythms. Approach the world “tentatively … listening carefully to what we don’t understand, rather than capturing, dissecting, analyzing and controlling it” (p. 70).
  - Expect the spiritual to appear through aesthetic experience: poetry, the chemistry of acids and alkalis, mudflat worms (p. 70). Include multiple cultures’ relations to the world and the spiritual (p. 69).
  - See each student as “only a limited manifestation of who this person is and can become”. Avoid labels (p. 70).
- **Check:** Is there a moment of wonder or meaning beyond utility? Are students free in how they respond?

### P2 Sense of coherence underlies healthy learning (pp. 72–75)

- **Rule (soul-economy):** Bring maximum learning with minimum means in minimum time, so that no student loses the overview or feels they don’t understand (Steiner, p. 72). Overload drains vitality.
- **Salutogenic indicators (Antonovsky):** **comprehensibility** (do they understand?), **manageability / self-efficacy** (can they cope?), **meaningfulness** (is it worth it to them?) (p. 73).
- **Fig. 2.1 levels (p. 74)**, where upper school ≈ Level 3:

| Component | L1 | L2 | **L3** |
|---|---|---|---|
| Comprehensibility | understanding in context (stories, primary tools, basic literacy) | exploring, researching, comparing | **developing powers of judgement; analysing and reflecting** |
| Manageability | participating, communicating, cooperating | producing and presenting | **creating, solving problems, making conceptual models** |
| Meaningfulness | empathy, identifying with the object of learning | taking responsibility, sharing | **taking one’s own position, doing good, helping others** |

- **Design:** For each step choose the most economical mode: direct experience, narrative and images, or texts and media (p. 74). Learning is like healthy nutrition, which activates the eater’s own activity (p. 74).
- **Check:** Name observable indicators of all three components for this lesson.

### P3 The teacher’s preparation shapes learning quality (pp. 75–79)

- **Rule:** Thorough preparation, the teacher’s own relationship to the material and knowledge of the learners. As a rough indication for the younger grades, ~3 hours of preparation per 30 minutes of intensive pupil learning (p. 76).
- **Design:**
  - **Select the most telling examples, the ones that need the least explanation.** Present in a lively way (p. 75).
  - If the lesson is well prepared, *don’t keep pointing out what is important*. Don’t draw the conclusions or give definitions or answers. Give time to explore, and help when needed (p. 77).
  - **“Make the horse thirsty and ensure the water is sweet”:** start from what students know, and add empathic, dramatic or surprising details for different learners (p. 77).
  - Avoid ready-made course materials as the main vehicle. The teacher’s own shaping is relational (Biesta: education is learning *from someone*) (p. 77). Software may suit *practice* but not lively presentation (p. 78).
  - Build anticipation, e.g. “we’ll understand this by …” (p. 76).
- **Check (p. 78):** level of self-activity the lesson prompts, and what is recalled next day, at block end and at the start of the next block.
- **Agent note:** Generated material is a *draft for the teacher to make their own*. Flag where the teacher’s personal relationship to the content needs to come in.

### P4 Readiness and time to learn (pp. 79–83)

- **Rule:** Work to a normative timetable, with tolerance for individuals. Ask “Is the school ready for this child?”, not “Is the child ready?” (p. 79). Prepare the room as a special place, part workshop, studio and sanctuary (p. 80).
- **Upper school:** Attunement is brief. Self-activity and alert interest matter more than routine (p. 82). Discuss the verse with older students so it doesn’t become empty routine (p. 80; the grade 12 vignette discussed its religious line with a multi-faith class, p. 63).
- **Check:** participation of *all* students. End-of-lesson state: refreshed, not exhausted.

### P5 Learning is rhythmical (pp. 83–94)

- **Rules:**
  - Daily, weekly, monthly and annual rhythms.
  - The primary rhythm is attunement → rich experience → forgetting → recall/sharing/concept → practice → growing ability → transformation (p. 83).
  - Learning yields **dispositions** (competences) that appear as performance in suitable situations. *How* a disposition is learned shapes the skill (p. 83).
- **Expansive vs defensive learning (Grotlüschen, pp. 88–89):**
  - Expansive learning comes from agency and the pursuit of biographical intentions and ideals.
  - Defensive learning means avoiding stress and chasing grades. It leads to alienation.
  - Prizes create a few winners and many losers. “I don’t have to be the best; I just want to do my best.” Design for **expansive** learning.
- **Language and art:** figurative, symbolic and metaphorical meaning counters meaninglessness. That is why art from many cultures is taught in grade 9 (p. 88). Holistic science gives glimpses of larger contexts (p. 88).
- **Aims:** socialisation, qualification and **subjectification** (Biesta), the coming into being of an agentic subject (pp. 93–94).

### P6 Learning processes are structured over time (pp. 94–98)

- See §2.4 for the overlapping cycle and §2.2 for Zech’s upper-school version.
- The teacher needs “both a systematic plan and an intuitive grasp of where the students are” (p. 95).

### P7 Block teaching strengthens dispositions (pp. 98–102)

- **Rule:** Immerse in one field for **3–4 weeks, ~90–100 min daily** (p. 98). Prolonged immersion → fallow / forgetting → recall reactivates skills. The aim is **long-term dispositions, not short-term memorising** (pp. 98–99).
- **Design:**
  - Aim for a *way of looking*: “to look at the earth geographically”, or the chemical perspective of substances and transformations (p. 99). The question per block: *what disposition to see the world will students take away?*
  - Use symbols and models consciously. The map-of-the-empire story: **reduce and abstract, then re-expand in exact imagination** grounded in rich sensorimotor experience (pp. 99–100).
  - Teach history through **symptomatic moments** and frames of reference (social structures, culture, economics) (p. 100).
  - Upper school is still **general and non-specialised** for all students to grade 12, but taught by specialists (p. 100). The most effective timetable puts all subjects in 45- or 90-minute blocks. Languages can run in 3–6-week blocks (pp. 101, 62).
- **Evidence cited:** Waldorf high school students with block teaching and fewer history lessons matched state peers, and showed somewhat higher historical understanding and narrative competence (van Norden & Schürenberg 2019, p. 99).

### P8 Direct experience is the basis for learning (pp. 102–103)

- **Rule:** Start as concretely as circumstances allow. Use excursions (farms, factories, galleries) and experiments. Activities must be **meaningful, not checklists** (“seen this, done that!”) (p. 102).
- **Rule:** Writing poems, stories and scenes makes later literary analysis “hands-on” (p. 102).
- **Rule:** Create **expectation and context beforehand**. “The aim is to direct attention, not say what it means” (p. 102).

### P9 Imagination through vivid pictorial description (pp. 103–105)

- **Rules:**
  - Vivid description activates imagination, feeling and bodily sensation. **Exact imagining requires an act of will** (p. 103).
  - Imagination needs real experience underneath it (p. 103).
  - Two kinds of description: **detailed** (to visualise) and **metaphorical/analogical** (hints at further meaning). Do not explain the analogy or moralise it; let the story speak, though students’ questions are welcome (p. 103).
- **Design:** Use narrative as the main mode, supported by pictures, maps, charts and film (p. 103). Interpreting images needs a hermeneutics of the image, and maps make most sense to people who have made maps (p. 105). Avoid mere show-and-tell (*Anschauungsunterricht*), which misses relational and moral meaning (pp. 104–105).
- **Imagination uses (p. 104):** exact recall, anticipating outcomes, artistic realisation, problem solving, practical work.

### P10 Phenomenology / symptomatology (pp. 105–116)

- **Rules:**
  - Encounter the phenomenon **first**, form a relationship, understand it in context, and only then construct the concept (p. 105).
  - The teacher also builds their own relationship to the phenomenon.
  - Encounter involves **interruption**, met with respectful, open interest. Learning is performative, aesthetic and transformative.
- **By subject (pp. 106–107):**
  - **Science:** phenomenological, moving from phenomenon to principle, law or category.
  - **Humanities and culture:** **symptomatological** and hermeneutic. Pick significant moments that reveal a process (emerging consciousness, emancipation, totalitarianism). For art: describe and experience → context (iconography, medium, cultural values) → ask “what comes to expression through this?”
- **Upper-school science:** “phenomena are explored, where possible **measured**, and data is collected, analysed and interpreted” (pp. 107–108). Middle school by contrast uses hands-on experiment, observation and description, e.g. *feel the rock and describe it* rather than *metamorphic rocks are formed by…* (p. 107).
- **Pedagogy of interruption (Biesta):** Faced with resistance, a student can overcome it (no change), avoid it (no change), or enter the **educational space** of dialogue and self-formation. Teachers can create conditions for this but cannot guarantee it (pp. 108–109). Projects, practicals and internships with **scaffolded reflection** support such “subjectification events” (p. 109).
- **Meyer-Drawe:** Learning begins where the known no longer suffices. It is messy, awkward, “getting tangled up with the world”, *pathei mathos* (learning through suffering) (p. 109).
- **Resonance triangle (Rosa):** teacher, student and object. The teacher is the tuning fork. Success = students captivated, at home, the content relevant. Failure = boredom, overload, external pressure → alienation and defensive learning (pp. 114–115).
- **Performative:** outcomes are partly unpredictable even for the teacher. Plan the situation, not what each person will experience (p. 113).
- **Teacher skills (p. 116):** enable students to *generate* knowledge rather than receive propositions. Start from the student’s current mode of understanding. Let the curriculum expand so knowledge grows with the students.

### P11 Teaching must be artistic (pp. 116–119)

- **Rule:** Skilled artistry = seeing all possibilities in the situation and composing methods into a whole for this group, here and now. No recipe. Decisions are made in the moment (p. 116).
- **Design:**
  - Teaching that “breathes” between concentration and relaxation (Wiechert, p. 117).
  - Rhythm *and* contrast. Every activity serves the aims, including participation and not only measurable outcomes (pp. 118–119).
  - Aesthetic care: environment, materials, careful presentation. “If things are worth doing, they are worth doing well”, meaning doing *our* best rather than being *the* best (p. 118).
  - Artistic teaching is unobtrusive and flowing, and not necessarily quiet (p. 118).
  - Use any established method, chosen by judgement of what helps this group at this moment (p. 119).

### P12 Self-activity is essential (pp. 119–122)

- **Rules:**
  - All learning is self-formation.
  - Students **document their own learning** in self-made main lesson books, folders or portfolios instead of textbooks (p. 120).
  - Upper school: a **portfolio** in which students select, present and *justify* their choice of work (p. 120).
  - Scaffold with models and exemplars (p. 120).
- **Three levels of application tasks (pp. 120–121):** use the same shared experience and recall for everyone, then differentiate the tasks:
  1. **Reproduce** what was presented.
  2. **Compare / transfer** to similar situations.
  3. **Open question:** the learner decides the right questions and how to answer them, or explores an entirely new aspect.
- **Differentiation dimensions (p. 121):**

| Dimension | Options |
|---|---|
| Content | individual goals; choice of theme; different tasks; individual emphasis within a topic |
| Pathway | more or less time; same time on open tasks; different presentation modes (oral, written, enacted, drawn, graphic) |
| Material | different difficulty; different media (text, image, film, graphic); different kinds of support |
| Social form | alone, pairs, groups; discussion; teams with roles |

- Inclusive and non-streamed: everyone does everything, in different ways (p. 120). Feedback is often **ipsative** (p. 121).

### P13 Development of the senses (pp. 122–126)

- **Rule:** Learning quality depends on the senses. Steiner names 12 senses in three groups (p. 122):
  - **Will / body senses:** touch, life, movement, balance.
  - **Feeling senses:** smell, taste, sight, warmth.
  - **Cognitive / social senses:** hearing, speech, thought, the other’s I.
- **Upper-school relevance:**
  - The **social senses** are schooled through social interaction (p. 124).
  - Perception is “an education of attention” (Gibson, p. 125). Mediate real-world encounter and help students tell the real from the virtual (p. 125).
  - Protect against activities that numb the senses to rich, subtle experience (p. 125).

### P14 The class is a learning community (pp. 132–135)

- **Rule:** Learning, identity, belonging and knowledge grow in a community of practice (Wenger). **Inclusion questions are participation questions** (p. 132).
- **Upper school:** students join specialists’ communities of practice. Rogoff’s modes run from cultural apprenticeship → guided participation → **participatory appropriation**, a process of “becoming rather than acquisition” (p. 133).
- **Rogoff’s criteria (pp. 133–134):**
  - all contribute to shared endeavours
  - eager to help and belong
  - activities organised so all can participate
  - the goal is participation
  - keen attention, with guidance spread across the community
  - communication grounded in shared activity, with narrative and drama conveying values
  - assessment for learning tied to the collective endeavour
- **Teacher skills (p. 135):**
  - build collaboration and respect
  - create a class narrative of belonging
  - design activities everyone can join at their own level
  - support participation
  - assess how far everyone is included

### P15 Assessment for learning (pp. 135–138)

- **Rules:**
  - Whole-person assessment covering socialisation, qualification and becoming a subject.
  - **Formative, ipsative and summative** modes, each for its own purpose.
  - Prefer **performance assessment and naturally occurring evidence**.
  - Feedback that is **kind, specific and helpful**.
  - Unobtrusive, embedded and unbureaucratic, yet documented (p. 135).
- **From grade 6 on**, give students a short written description of the block's content and assessment criteria, showing possible levels of outcome. At block end, give a summary of attainment against those criteria plus the next learning step (p. 138). The upper-school vignettes do exactly this (block sheet with aims, forms of learning, criteria, portfolio requirements; pp. 59–63).
- **Assessable values (p. 137):**
  1. constructive participation, enabling others to participate
  2. working with others
  3. listening to others, not just the teacher
  4. articulating thoughts, feelings and intentions intelligibly
  5. understanding shown in words, text and images
  6. subject skills applied
  7. learning from mistakes
  8. independent and team project work
  9. self-assessment and goal-setting
  10. aesthetic presentation
  11. motivation shown in behaviour
- **Self-assessment:** upper-school students can apply the teachers’ own criteria (p. 137).
- **Modes (p. 137):** observation and monitoring, projects, checklists, feedback on main lesson books, presentations and portfolios, written block tests, small-group oral exams, self-evaluation.
- **Planning documents (p. 138):**
  - **Annual class plan:** all subjects, for synergies and to avoid overlap.
  - **Block plan:** content, skills framework, new knowledge with retrieval of embodied knowledge, capacities framework, dispositions practised.
  - **Lesson plan:** structure, sequence, content, tasks.
  - All are reviewed, with work samples from weak, typical and strong students.

### P17 Curriculum maps possible learning situations to developmental tasks (pp. 142–152)

- **Rules:**
  - Curriculum = *everything* students experience, including how and why, the teacher as a person and the architecture (Dahlin, p. 143).
  - It answers how pedagogy responds to **developmental tasks** (p. 142).
  - It has three dimensions:
    - **Horizontal:** one school year linked by guiding ideas.
    - **Vertical:** a subject's progression from grade 1 to grade 12.
    - **Spiral:** key concepts (wholeness, interrelatedness, transformation, emancipation…) revisited as living concepts.
  - **There is no canonical Waldorf curriculum; curriculum is emergent** (p. 142).
- **Aims (Bransby & Rawson, p. 143):**
  - **Qualification:** dispositions, skills, knowledge and cultural capital for work and civil society.
  - **Socialisation:** participating in a multicultural society, coherent identities.
  - **Individuation:** agency, judgement, sense of coherence, responsibility, ethics, life orientation, creativity, a relationship to spirituality.
- **Inputs to curriculum (p. 144):**
  - the generative principles
  - generalised maturational pathways
  - **local social and statutory expectations** (→ Thai indicators belong here)
  - individual learning needs
- **Skill growth (p. 145):** potentialities → skills (knowledgeable action with purpose) → abilities → lifelong capacities. Track skills (e.g. literacy) **across subjects**.
- **Decolonise and localise (pp. 150–151):**
  - Traditional Waldorf curricula are Eurocentric.
  - Teach history **symptomatologically** and **globally from a local perspective**, emphasising intercultural contact. Rawson’s example for Asia: the Silk Roads.
  - Avoid essentialised “cultural epochs”.
  - Consider place-based pedagogy (Boland’s “Waldorf 2.0”).

## 4. Subject-specific principles from the upper-school vignettes

**Art study (grade 9, pp. 59–62)**
- Symptomatic, not a comprehensive survey: art expresses how people relate to world and self in a given time and place.
- **Accurate observation → description → comparison → only then judgement.** This trains hermeneutic method without naming it and counters adolescent over-subjectivity.
- Study techniques, materials, gestures, embodied experience and context. *Enact* sculptures' poses to feel balance and tension.
- Studying art is separate from practising fine art.
- Block sheet: aims, forms of learning, portfolio contents (sketches, one full copy, three picture descriptions, notes on context, a formal essay), assessment criteria.

**Foreign language (grade 12, pp. 62–64)**
- 90-minute blocks over 3 weeks, each with a theme (e.g. post-colonial short stories, Shakespeare performed in scenes).
- Conducted entirely in the target language.
- Lesson sequence: small talk in pairs → groups clarify understanding of the text → class discussion of themes → two students summarise → choice of tasks (stylistic analysis / social-context description / creative writing) → self-paced quiet work with individual feedback.
- Criteria: participation, using the target language at all times, three texts (factual, analytical, creative), corrections, unassessed reflection.
- Principles: holistic (body, feeling, ideas); immersion in meaningful social practice; orality remains core; language as a *medium* for age-sensitive content such as identity issues and other cultures; authentic literature; aesthetic sensibility.

**Craft and work (grade 10 with grade 5, pp. 57–59)**
- Handcrafts educate the will through meaningful engagement with materials.
- Craft reconnects people with nature. Work transforms both matter and the person.
- Understanding hand technology is a conceptual basis for digital technology.
- Do things well for their own sake.
- Grade 10 role: survey to professional standards; plan and break down tasks; lead mixed-age teams.

**Science, carried from grade 7 (p. 57)**
- Phenomena are experienced and described before definitions or laws.
- First-hand experience.
- Leave the question open overnight. The grade 7 teacher moved on without resolving the mirror question.

## 5. Generation checklist (apply to every block/lesson draft)

> Agent synthesis of the principles above, for use as a QA gate. Each item cites its principle.

1. **Developmental fit:** Does the work call for students' *own judgement* and *individual positioning*? Does it touch adolescent identity, ideals or biographical concerns? (§1, P5)
2. **Phenomenon first:** Does each concept arise from an encounter with a phenomenon, or a symptomatic moment, *before* terms, laws or definitions? In science, are measurement and data analysis included where possible? (P8, P10)
3. **Overnight rhythm:** Is a new experience left un-analysed until the next lesson? Does each lesson open with open-question recall of yesterday? Do the E→R→C→A cycles overlap across the week? (P5, P6)
4. **Concept formation:** Are concepts co-constructed and *characterised* in dialogue, with technical terms added afterwards? Is closure deliberately left open where that is fruitful? (P5, P6, P10)
5. **Disposition target:** Does the block plan name the *way of seeing* or disposition being grown, not just the content? (P7)
6. **Interruption:** Is there at least one planned encounter likely to puzzle, surprise or resist (anomaly, dilemma, discrepant event, unfamiliar culture or text), plus scaffolded reflection on it? (P10)
7. **Imagination:** Where direct experience is impossible, is there vivid, exact narrative or imagery? Are analogies left unexplained? (P9)
8. **Coherence:** Comprehensible, manageable and meaningful, at Level 3 (judgement, modelling, own position)? Is the means economical, with no overload? (P2)
9. **Rhythm and artistry:** Are activity, mood and social form varied? Is there a sonata-like arc? Do students end energised, not drained? Is the structure clear but flexible, with alternatives? (P4, P11)
10. **Self-activity:** Do students document their own learning (main lesson book, portfolio)? Do application tasks run at three levels (reproduce / transfer / open)? Is work differentiated by content, pathway, material and social form? (P12)
11. **Community and participation:** Can every student participate? Are there collaborative roles and a sense of shared endeavour? (P14)
12. **Assessment for learning:** Is there a block sheet (aims, forms of learning, criteria, outcome levels)? Is evidence naturally occurring and performance-based? Is feedback ipsative where possible, with a closing summary and next step? (P15)
13. **Meaning beyond utility:** Is there a moment of wonder, reverence or intrinsic value? Are multiple cultural perspectives present without imposing a worldview? (P1, P17)
14. **Local and statutory layer:** Are local standards (e.g. Thai indicators) mapped at meso level *without* letting them dictate a defensive, test-driven mode? Are topics localised, with global and intercultural links? (P17)
15. **Teacher ownership:** Is the output framed as material for the teacher to make their own, with notes on where their judgement and relationship to the content come in? (P3, P11)

## 6. Anti-patterns (what the principles rule out)

| Anti-pattern | Contradicts |
|---|---|
| Definition, law or formula first, then examples | P10 |
| Analysing an experience exhaustively in the same lesson | P5 (forgetting) |
| Recall run as a quiz with one right answer | P5 |
| Teacher spells out what to notice, the moral, the conclusion | P3, P9 |
| Show-and-tell: picture plus label as understanding | P9 |
| Fixed script or standard plan applied regardless of the class | §0, P11 |
| Textbook or worksheet as the main record instead of student-made documentation | P12 |
| Prizes, rankings, grade-chasing as motivation | P5 (defensive learning) |
| One task for all with no differentiation; or streaming | P12, P14 |
| Fragmented short lessons with no immersion | P7 |
| Content as mere facts, no disposition or way of seeing | P7 |
| Eurocentric canon or essentialised “cultural epochs” | P17 |
| Lesson that leaves students exhausted or stressed | P2, P4 |
| Field trip as a checklist (“seen this, done that”) | P8 |
