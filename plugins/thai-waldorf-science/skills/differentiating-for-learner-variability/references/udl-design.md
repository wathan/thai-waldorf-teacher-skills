# UDL design: barriers, shared task, targeted supports

How `differentiating-for-learner-variability` turns a plan into one differentiation plan. Draws on CAST's UDL guidelines (Rose & Meyer, 2002; CAST, 2018) as summarised in the education-agent-skills inclusive-design skills, on Rawson (2021) P12 for the Waldorf upper-school view, and on the k12 differentiation skill's single plan with shared content written once.

## Principles

- Variability is the norm. Design for the whole range up front; do not retrofit after students fail.
- Same destination, different routes: every option keeps the core learning intention and its cognitive demand. Scaffolds reduce barriers of access and language, never the thinking. An option that would lower the demand is flagged, not offered as equivalent.
- Specific beats generic: "a sentence frame for the written explanation: ข้อมูลแสดงว่า … เพราะ …" is a design change; "add scaffolding" is not.
- Fewer, better options: two or three per session, chosen for this class. Too many choices are themselves a barrier for students with attention or executive-function needs.
- Inclusive and non-streamed (Rawson P12): everyone does everything, in different ways; differentiate by content emphasis, pathway, material and social form.

## Anticipating barriers

For each session or task part, ask where the plan as written would stop some students. Record each barrier in a table:

| Where (session, part) | Barrier (a concrete feature) | Affects (by need) | Severity | Removed by design, or needs individual/specialist support |
|---|---|---|---|---|

Look in five places:

- **Engagement:** no choice or relevance, public performance or timed formats that raise anxiety, long stretches with no checkpoint, unclear purpose of the day.
- **Representation:** dense text, a single channel (audio only, text only), assumed background knowledge, academic vocabulary and grammar (see the language-supports reference), data shown in one form only.
- **Action and expression:** extended writing when the goal is not writing, oral performance, planning and organising a multi-step task, fine-motor or equipment demands.
- **Environment:** noise during practical work, seating and sightlines for a demonstration, outdoor or field conditions, group dynamics.
- **Assessment format:** a format that tests something other than the learning intention (e.g. a 200-word written explanation where the intention is the scientific explanation, not the writing).

Severity: high (likely to stop the task), medium (slows or overloads), low (friction). Prioritise the three to five highest-impact design changes.

## The shared task

Keep the plan's task as the one task for everyone. Redesign it so that every student can start and every student can be stretched:

- **Ways in (representation):** the experience through more than one sense; data as table, graph and picture; key words written, drawn and spoken; a short worked example or model answer structure; chunked text with headings or a read-aloud option.
- **Ways to take part and show (action and expression):** talk in pairs before writing; a choice of record in the main lesson book (labelled drawing, diagram, table, text); a visible step list for multi-step work; sentence frames (language-supports reference).
- **Ways to stay engaged:** individual thinking time before sharing; clear roles in groups; a visible plan for the session; a choice of example or context within the same idea.
- **Room to extend, inside the same task:** a further question on the same idea ("what would happen if…", "which evidence would change your mind?"), a richer dataset, an anomaly to explain, a transfer to a second case. Extension deepens the intention; it is never a separate topic or extra quantity of the same work.

For a main lesson block keep the two-day rhythm: options belong to Recall and concept formation, practice and the close; the day's new experience is offered through several senses but not analysed the same day; Recall stays open questions.

## Targeted supports by need

Only for needs in the class profile with a count above zero (or described in the request). Each support refers to the shared task by name ("in Tuesday's written explanation, …") instead of restating it.

| Need (profile row) | Typical targeted supports |
|---|---|
| Learning differences (reading-related) | read-aloud or audio of key text; text chunked with a key-sentence highlight; reduced copying (printed data, partially labelled diagrams); extra time for writing; spoken or drawn explanation accepted as evidence |
| Learning differences (attention) | the task split into short steps with a check-in after each; a visible step list or timer; a movement or role-based part; seating away from distraction; one choice, not many |
| Language of instruction as an additional language | pre-taught Tier 3 and key Tier 2 words with pictures; bilingual word list (home language as the teacher can provide); graded sentence frames; partner who shares a language for talk before writing; oral rehearsal before writing (language-supports reference) |
| Extension | the extension prompts inside the shared task; a role that requires justification (e.g. critic of the class model); open questions where the student frames the question (Rawson's third level) |
| Other access needs (hearing) | captions or a transcript for audio or film; seating with a clear view of the speaker; key instructions written on the board |
| Other access needs (vision, physical, health) | enlarged or tactile materials; adapted equipment or a partner role for handling; rest points; field-trip access checked in advance |

## Individual adjustments and specialist support

Add an individual adjustment only when the teacher describes a need the shared task and targeted supports cannot meet. Write it by need ("one student with a hearing need"), never by name or identifying detail. Be honest about limits: UDL is the floor, not the whole of support. Where a barrier needs specialist assessment, a learning-support colleague or family input, say so plainly instead of implying the plan solves it.

## Tiers, when they are used

Only when the teacher asks, or a barrier cannot be met inside one shared task (state which). Then:

- Write the shared parts once (learning intention, experience, Recall and concept formation, the core question), then only what differs per tier.
- Every tier asks for the same thinking (e.g. every tier explains why, not only what).
- Name tiers by the support or route ("with a guided step list", "with frames", "open route"), not by ability ("low", "weak", "below"). If the teacher used ability labels, keep them only as their request's names in one line and use support names in the plan.
- Students choose or move between tiers; never assign a tier to a student or a need in the plan.
- Rawson's three levels of application (reproduce, transfer, open question) may serve as the tiers when they fit.
