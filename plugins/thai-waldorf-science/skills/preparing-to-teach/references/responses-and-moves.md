# Anticipated responses and dialogic moves

How to anticipate what students will say in a session and pair each likely response with a follow-up move that builds on it. The moves follow dialogic teaching (Alexander; Mercer's exploratory talk; Michaels and colleagues' accountable talk moves): the teacher's next words use a student's idea to move the class's thinking on, rather than judging it.

## Anticipating responses

Work from the plan, not from a generic topic list:

- **What students met:** yesterday's experience (for Recall) and today's (for New Content), exactly as the plans describe them.
- **What the class is working toward:** the target idea named in the Recall cell and the block plan. Likely responses sit between what students already hold and that idea.
- **What the main lesson block assumes:** the prerequisite ideas. A response that shows a prerequisite idea missing is worth a move of its own.
- **Known misconceptions for this science content**, phrased as a student would say them (e.g. "plants get their food from the soil", "heavier things fall faster", "the dissolved salt is gone"). Prefer two or three likely ones to a long list.
- **Students' own questions** on the driving question board record; the session may be where some of them come back.
- **Learner variability:** students learning in an additional language may give a correct idea in few or everyday words; plan a move that takes the idea seriously before the wording.

For each part with talk in it, give two or three anticipated responses: at least one partly right, one misconception, and, at Recall, one "I don't remember" or a bare description.

## The moves

| Move | What it does | Example wording |
|---|---|---|
| Revoicing | Makes the idea public and checks it | "So you're saying the air comes from where it is cooler. Have I got that right?" |
| Pressing for reasoning | Asks why the student thinks it | "What makes you think that?" |
| Pressing for evidence | Ties the claim to what was seen | "What did you see yesterday that fits that?" |
| Inviting others | Turns one answer into class talk | "Who sees it differently?" · "Who can add to that?" |
| Challenging | Offers a case that tests the idea | "Then what would happen at night, when the land cools?" |
| Building on | Links the idea to another student's, to the board or to earlier sessions | "That connects to a question on our board. Which one?" |
| Waiting and writing | Gives everyone thinking time first | "Write your answer in one sentence first; then tell your neighbour." |

Give each move as the teacher would say it, one or two sentences, and where useful a short second turn ("If they say …, ask …").

## Matching the move to the response

- **Partly right:** revoice, then press for the missing part.
- **Misconception:** revoice so the claim is visible, then press for evidence or challenge with a case from the experience; or invite others. Never correct it by telling.
- **Correct but thin:** press for reasoning, or challenge to see if it holds.
- **Unclear or few words:** revoice tentatively ("Are you saying …?"), offer to sketch it, or let the student say it in their first language to a partner first.
- **"I don't remember":** go back to the senses and the main lesson book drawing ("What did you hear? Look at your drawing: what is the first thing you drew?"), then invite others.
- **Strong:** build on it and invite others to test it; do not stop the talk there.

## Part by part

- **Rhythmic Time, Maths/Literacy:** usually no anticipated responses needed; one line at most.
- **Recall (two-day rhythm):** open questions about yesterday's experience as it was lived: what students noticed, in what order, what surprised them. Responses here are descriptions; moves draw out more detail and invite others. No concept questions yet.
- **Concept formation (same row, after Recall):** students take an individual position (written or sketched first), then the class integrates positions. Anticipate the positions likely to be taken, including misconceptions, and give moves that let the class weigh them against the experience. The explanatory model stays with the students: never script the concept they must reach.
- **Practice:** anticipate where students get stuck starting the shared task and where some finish early; give a move for each (a starting question, an extension question).
- **New Content (today's new experience):** anticipate what students notice and ask. Moves only open noticing and wondering: "What do you notice?", "Draw exactly what you see.", "Write that question down; tomorrow we start there." When a student asks why or offers an explanation, hold it for tomorrow without judging it. Never explain.
- **Four-day cycle:** recall of E(n−1) gets description moves only; concept formation on E(n−2) gets the concept moves above; application of an earlier concept is Practice.
- **5E session:** Engage and Explore get elicitation and noticing moves; Explain gets the concept-formation moves, with students' explanations first; Elaborate is Practice; Evaluate follows the session's own assessment.

## Rules

- No move evaluates ("Good", "Not quite", "Exactly"), openly or by confirming: revoice as the student's idea ("So you think the light made it grow?"), never as the teacher's agreement ("So the light matters.").
- No move tells students the target idea or a predetermined concept, and no leading question that has only one acceptable answer.
- Recall is never a quiz: no closed questions with one right answer.
- At least one quieter channel per discussion part (write first, pairs, sketch) for students less sure in talk or in the language of instruction.
- No student names in examples or moves: "Who sees it differently?", never "Who agrees with Ploy?".
