# Audit rules: domains, reach, closures and the special cases

How to read a plan for epistemic decisions. An epistemic decision is a classroom decision that drives knowledge-building and whose authorship can rest with the teacher or the students, in three domains (Ko & Krist, 2019). An opening hands a decision to students; a closure keeps it with the teacher. Closures are not faults: a good design closes some decisions deliberately so that others can open. The audit makes every closure visible with its reason so that each one is a choice.

## The three domains

| Domain | The decision is about | Opened when the plan has students… | Closed when the plan has the teacher… |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Investigation aim** | What the class is trying to figure out, which question to pursue next | write and cluster their own questions (driving question board); reword the driving question; choose which question or unit comes next; set a sub-question for their group | set the question, the next unit or the focus; give the task's aim ready-made |
| **Investigation method** | How to find out: what to observe or measure, what to vary, which data, how to record and analyse | decide what to test or change, plan their own trial, choose the data or the way to record, decide how to compare results | give the procedure, the worksheet steps, the variables, the data set, the way to record |
| **Explanatory model** | What explains the phenomenon, and what counts as good evidence or a good explanation | take individual positions (claims, sketch models, own-words explanations) that the class compares and integrates; revise models; agree their own criteria for a good model or good evidence; judge between explanations | state the concept, definition, model or answer; give the criteria for a good explanation; settle disagreements by telling |

A session can open one domain and close another (e.g. a given procedure, then students' own explanation of the result). Report each decision in the domain it belongs to; one sentence each.

## Reach

Every opening gets a reach: how far the students' decision shapes what follows.

- **This session**: the decision is used up in the session (a group's choice of how to record today).
- **Next sessions**: name them (e.g. "into day 5", "sessions 4–5").
- **Rest of the unit**: e.g. the class's own criteria used for every model in the unit.
- **Rest of the sequence**: decisions at the highest curricular level, such as the driving question and the driving question board, which frame every later session.

Higher-level decisions (anchoring phenomenon, driving question, order of units) have the widest reach; say so when comparing openings. A closure's reach can be noted the same way when it matters (a fixed sequence closes the order for the whole sequence).

## Reasons for closures

Give every closure one reason. Use the plan's own reason where it states one (e.g. a unit plan's "Explain-phase closures"); otherwise give the most likely design reason and mark it **(inferred)**. Typical reasons:

- **Anchoring phenomenon**: the teacher's deliberate choice that opens the sequence; students' questions are elicited from it.
- **Prerequisite idea**: assumed and taught beforehand, so it is not for the class to figure out.
- **Fixed sequence**: the teacher set the unit order in advance; the driving question board frames ideas, not order.
- **Canonical term, formula or convention**: introduced after students' own explanation so the class can name what it found and meet the indicator's wording.
- **Skill or procedure taught explicitly**: reading a meter, a calculation, a graph convention, needed before students can investigate or apply.
- **Safety, equipment or time**: the procedure or materials are set for safety, availability or the session's length.
- **Indicator demands it**: a Thai indicator names the content, quantity or method (quote the code).
- **Recall stays on yesterday's experience**: the two-day rhythm fixes what Recall starts from.

When no defensible reason appears (e.g. a worksheet procedure where the class could plan its own, a teacher-chosen question where the board could choose), write **closure without a reason** and leave the decision to the teacher. Do not invent a reason to make the plan look deliberate.

## Special rules

1. **The anchoring phenomenon is always a deliberate closure** (investigation aim, reach: the whole sequence, reason: the teacher's choice). Report it first, never as a weakness, and never suggest opening it.
2. **Concept formation expects the explanatory-model domain open.** In the two-day rhythm, concept formation is each student's own position integrated into the class's understanding (ADR-0003). When the plan has the teacher state the concept, students copy a given definition, the objective reads "students learn that…", or the target idea is written as the sentence students must reach, **flag it as a scripted concept**: name the session, quote the wording, and say the explanatory-model domain is expected open there. Terms added after the class's own positions are a closure with a reason, not a scripted concept.
3. **Explain in subject lessons.** Students explaining from their own evidence is an opening in the explanatory-model domain. What the teacher then introduces (terms, formulae, the accepted explanation) is a list of **Explain-phase closures**, each with its reason. An Explain where the teacher gives the concept before students have explained their evidence is flagged as a scripted concept.
4. **Student-set criteria are an opening** in the explanatory-model domain (the class decides what counts as good evidence or a good explanation), with the reach of their use. Criteria the teacher hands out are a closure (reason usually: a scored assessment or rubric).
5. **Assessment as learning is not uptake** and not an opening: exit slips, self-assessment against given criteria, reflections. Name them under "not counted" with one line: they concern individual learning, not the class's knowledge work. (Self-assessment against criteria the class set itself counts once, as the criteria-setting opening, not again.)
6. **New Content left un-analysed is not a closure** of the explanatory model: explanation is deferred to tomorrow's concept formation. Choosing the experience is the teacher's (a closure in investigation aim or method with reason: the fixed sequence or the design's next experience; in an anticipated route it may follow the board's choice).
7. **Recall's open questions** neither open nor close a decision on their own; what follows (positions, questions to the board) is what counts.
8. **Retrieval practice and graded practice** apply what was already formed: usually closed method (reason: consolidation, procedure practice); not a scripted concept.

## Reading each kind of plan

- **Main lesson block, week or day plans:** one row or day plan per session. The block plan's Storyline design section gives the sequence-level decisions. Two-day rhythm parts: Recall + concept formation (explanatory model expected open), Reflection (assessment as learning), Practice (often closed method; open if students choose how), New Content (rule 6), Close.
- **Anticipated route:** the board's choice of the next interchangeable unit is an opening in investigation aim with reach to the rest of the sequence; the fixed anchor units stay closures with reasons.
- **Subject lesson, unit and cycle plans:** one session table per session; the unit plan holds the sequence-level decisions and any stated Explain-phase closures. Engage (phenomenon or challenge chosen by the teacher: closure; students' noticing and wondering: an opening in investigation aim only if their questions shape what is investigated), Explore (method open or closed), Explain (rule 3), Elaborate (application), Evaluate (assessment for learning; who sets the criteria, rule 4).
- **A session described in the request:** audit it as described; say what the description leaves unclear rather than guess.

## Design only, never uptake

The audit reads plans. It cannot see whether students took up an opening, whose ideas shaped the class's model, or how talk went. Write "the plan opens", "the design hands students", "planned"; never "students decided", "students took up", "the class owned". End the report with one line: uptake can only be seen in teaching, not in a plan. Never score students or the plan.

## Report outline

```markdown
# Epistemic agency audit: <part audited>

Audited from: <plans, in plain words> · Kind: <main lesson block, two-day rhythm | subject lesson, 5E inquiry> · Date: <date>

## Summary

<One paragraph: where the plan opens decisions and with what reach, the deliberate closures, the flags.>

## Sequence-level decisions

| Decision | Domain | Opened or closed | Reach or reason |
|---|---|---|---|
| Anchoring phenomenon: … | Investigation aim | Closed (deliberate) | The teacher's choice that opens the sequence; reach: whole sequence |
| Driving question (authorship: …) | Investigation aim | … | … |

## Session by session

| Session | Investigation aim | Investigation method | Explanatory model |
|---|---|---|---|
| Day 2 (Tue) | Opened: students' questions cluster into the driving question board (reach: rest of the block) | Not in this session | Opened: individual sketch models compared as a class (reach: revised day 3 onward) |
| Day 3 (Wed) | … | Closed: … — reason: … (inferred) | **Flag: scripted concept** — "…" |

## Flags

- Scripted concept: <session, quoted wording, why the domain is expected open>
- Closure without a reason: <session, decision>

## Explain-phase closures (subject lessons)

- <session>: <what the teacher introduces> — <reason>

## Not counted

- <session>: <exit slip / self-assessment> — assessment as learning, not uptake.

## Design only

This audit reads the plan. Whether students take up these openings can only be seen in teaching.
```

Keep cells short (a decision and its reach or reason). Add "## Ways to open more" only when the teacher asked (see opening-more.md).
