# Assessment for learning

Evidence the teacher gathers during a teaching sequence to decide the next instructional step (glossary). Every item names what the teacher looks for and what they do next. Never scored.

## Hinge questions

A hinge question sits at the point where the next step depends on whether the class holds an idea (after hinge-question-designer).

1. **Name the idea** the next part builds on: the target idea formed so far, never the next one (target ideas are figured out, not tested in advance).
2. **List the likely misconceptions** for this idea and this grade, before writing options: what students commonly think instead, and why it is plausible to them. Use the class's own positions from concept formation where the plan records them.
3. **Write the question**: one stem, three or four options, one correct and each other option the answer a student with one named misconception would choose. No trick wording, no "all of the above". Answerable in under a minute. In the language of student materials.
4. **Route every response** to a next step, in a table:

| Option | What choosing it suggests | Next step |
|---|---|---|
| A | Holds the target idea | Move on to Practice |
| B | Misconception: … | Five-minute return to the experience or data that contradicts it, then ask again |
| C | Misconception: … | Pair talk comparing B and C with the evidence, then a second hinge question |

Add the decision rule: roughly how many correct answers mean moving on, and what to do with the few who did not (a quick word during Practice, not holding the class back).

5. **Collect all at once**: letter cards or mini-whiteboards shown together, or a folded slip. It is a low-stakes check; nobody is singled out.

## Misconception-based checks

The same logic in other forms (after k12 check-for-understanding and checking-for-understanding-protocol-designer): a prediction ("What will happen if…? Sketch it"), a "which explanation do you agree with?" between two student-like explanations, an odd-one-out, or a sketch model to annotate. Each likely response, drawn from a named misconception, is routed to a next step exactly as above. Plan one or two per week in a main lesson block, at concept formation or before a Practice that depends on the idea; one per 5E cycle at the Explain–Elaborate turn.

## Main lesson book as evidence

The main lesson book is naturally occurring evidence (Rawson P15): plan what the teacher reads in it, not an extra task. For each day or week name:

- **which entry** (the recall sketch, the position taken in concept formation, the Practice table or write-up);
- **what the teacher looks for**, tied to the criteria students know (a block sheet's criteria where the course has one) and to the target idea;
- **what they do next** with what they find (a comment that asks a question, a point to pick up at tomorrow's Recall, a small group during Practice).

Feedback is kind, specific and helpful, and ipsative where possible (compared with the student's own earlier work).

## Recall and retrieval

- **Main lesson Recall** is assessment for learning through open questions only. The teacher listens for what students noticed, what they missed and what they already explain; that shapes concept formation. Never a quiz, a test or closed questions with one right answer, and never scored.
- **Retrieval practice** (after retrieval-practice-generator) belongs to subject lessons: two to five low-stakes questions at the start of a short session on earlier cycles, spaced over weeks, answered privately and checked by the student. Never scored. In a main lesson block it is used only in Practice on concepts formed on earlier days, never at Recall.

## Choosing a technique

After formative-assessment-technique-selector, pick by the decision the teacher must make:

| The teacher needs to know | Technique |
|---|---|
| Can the class move on? | Hinge question with all-at-once responses |
| What does each student think now? | Prediction or sketch model in the main lesson book, read after the session |
| Which misconception is common? | "Which explanation do you agree with?" with student-like options |
| Is a skill secure before practice is let go? | Check for understanding on one worked step (subject lessons: in Explain) |
| What has stuck from earlier weeks? | Retrieval practice (subject lessons) |

Fewer, well-placed checks beat many: one or two items per session.
